# Consciousness Beyond Humans: Who Else Thinks, Feels, and Knows?

On July 7, 2012, a group of neuroscientists at Churchill College, Cambridge signed a declaration that challenged one of the oldest assumptions in Western philosophy. The **Cambridge Declaration on Consciousness** stated: "The weight of evidence indicates that humans are not unique in possessing the neurological substrates that generate consciousness."

Signatories: Philip Low, Jaak Panksepp, Diana Reiss, David Edelman, Bruno Van Swinderen, and Christof Koch. The declaration explicitly named all mammals, all birds, and many other creatures — including octopuses — as bearers of conscious states.

Twelve years later, in April 2024, the **New York Declaration on Animal Consciousness** went further. And the research between these two landmarks has not simplified the picture — it has radicalized it.

## From Cambridge to New York: Scientific Consensus Shifts

The Cambridge Declaration (2012) opened a door. Its impact was substantial: the journal *Animal Sentience* was founded in 2016. The EU, UK, New Zealand, and parts of Australia recognized animal sentience in legislation. The debate shifted from **whether** animals are conscious to **which** animals and **what form** their consciousness takes.

The **New York Declaration** (April 2024, initiated by Jeff Sebo, Kristin Andrews, and Jonathan Birch) drew the circle wider:

1. **Strong scientific support** for conscious experience in all mammals and birds.
2. **Realistic possibility** of consciousness in **all vertebrates** and many **invertebrates** (cephalopods, decapod crustaceans, insects).
3. **Ethical imperative**: where realistic possibility of consciousness exists, ignoring it in decisions affecting that animal is irresponsible.

Forty initial signatories including Christof Koch, Anil Seth, David Chalmers, and Lars Chittka. A scientific consensus that shifted fundamentally in under a decade.

## The Octopus: An Alien Consciousness on Earth

If one organism demonstrates that consciousness is not bound to a single architecture, it is the octopus.

**500 million neurons**, approximately 350 million in the arms. Each arm contains a brachial ganglion capable of autonomous sensory processing and motor control (Hochner, 2012, *Current Biology*). There is no single "control center" — it is a **decentralized neural network** that coordinates without central authority.

### Pain: Not Just Reflexes

Robyn Crook (2021, *iScience*) showed that pygmy octopuses experience **tonic and emotional pain**. In conditioned place preference assays, animals avoided pain-associated contexts and preferred locations where they received pain relief via lidocaine. This is not reflex — it is suffering avoidance through memory.

### Tool Use

Finn et al. (2009, *Current Biology*) documented *Amphioctopus marginatus* carrying coconut shell halves as portable shelters — the **first recorded invertebrate tool use**.

Peter Godfrey-Smith argued in *Other Minds* (2016) that cephalopods represent an independent experiment in the evolution of large brains and complex behavior — diverging from the vertebrate lineage 600 million years ago.

## Crows: Metacognition and Subjective Experience

Corvids — crows, ravens, jays — are among the most cognitively complex non-mammals. Recent research goes beyond cognitive performance to reveal **indicators of subjective experience**.

### Neural Correlates of Consciousness

Nieder et al. (2020, *Science*) recorded single-neuron responses in carrion crows during a visual detection task. Neurons in the nidopallium caudolaterale (NCL) — functionally equivalent to the mammalian prefrontal cortex — encoded the bird's **subjective perception** rather than physical stimulus properties.

This was the **first demonstration of a neural correlate of sensory consciousness in a non-mammalian species**.

### Knowing What You Know

Lambert et al. (2019, *Animal Cognition*) showed ravens selectively check only unknown hiding spots while going directly to known food locations — evidence they monitor their own knowledge state. Clayton & Dickinson (1998, *Nature*) documented mental time travel in scrub jays.

A 2025 study in *Animal Cognition* applied Birch's 5-dimensional framework for animal consciousness to corvids, concluding they satisfy multiple criteria for conscious experience.

## Bees: Miniature Consciousness with Dopamine

Lars Chittka, professor at Queen Mary University of London, published *The Mind of a Bee* (Princeton University Press, 2022) — fundamentally challenging the boundary between "simple" and "conscious."

Key findings across decades of research:

- Bees solve problems by **thinking, not trial and error**
- Bees display **metacognition** — they "know what they know"
- Bees show individual personality differences
- Bees exhibit **play behavior**: Dona et al. (2022, *Animal Behaviour*) documented bumblebees rolling small wooden balls with no food reward
- Bees show **anxiety-like states** after simulated predator attacks
- Bees have dopamine-dependent positive emotion-like states

Chittka's personal assessment: "Taking all of this together, I am now about 80% certain that they are conscious, sentient beings."

## Slime Mold: Intelligence Without a Single Nerve Cell

*Physarum polycephalum* — a single-celled organism that solves mazes, optimizes railway networks, and makes irrational decisions like humans.

**Nakagaki et al. (2000, *Nature*)**: Slime mold spread through a labyrinth, retracted from dead ends, and left a single optimal tube connecting two food sources.

**Tero et al. (2010, *Science*)**: Slime mold placed on a map of Tokyo with oat flakes at city locations created a network with **comparable efficiency, fault tolerance, and cost** to the actual Tokyo rail system.

Reid et al. (2012, *PNAS*): slime mold makes irrational decisions when presented with decoy options — a pattern **identical to human and animal decision-making biases**, suggesting shared computational principles.

## Sperm Whales: Combinatorial Language and Cultural Identity

Sharma et al. (2024, *Nature Communications*) revealed hundreds of distinct codas in sperm whale communication with **combinatorial structure** — click number, timing, and conversational context all carry meaning.

Rendell et al. (2022, *PNAS*) showed that coda dialects function as cultural identity markers between whale clans — "symbolic marking in non-human cultures."

Dolphins passed mirror self-recognition tests at **seven months** — earlier than human children (Reiss et al., 2018, *PLOS ONE*). Google's **DolphinGemma** (2025) is an AI model trained on dolphin vocalizations to decode communicative patterns.

## The Theories: IIT and Global Workspace

How do we explain consciousness across such different organisms?

**Integrated Information Theory (IIT 4.0)**, published by Tononi et al. in *PLoS Computational Biology* (2023), can in principle be applied to **any system of units in a state**. The measure Phi quantifies integrated information. Any system with Phi > 0 possesses some degree of consciousness — including organisms with radically different neural architectures.

**Global Workspace Theory (GWT)**, formulated by Bernard Baars (1988), proposes consciousness arises from **global availability of information**. Birch (2020) argued that simpler workspace architectures might support simpler forms of consciousness. The key is not the specifically human implementation, but the principle of information distribution.

Both theories are contested — but both productively shift the question from "Does this animal have a brain like ours?" to "Does this system process information in a way that can generate consciousness?"

## The Map Expands

The research of recent years reveals: consciousness is not a binary switch. It is a **spectrum**, a gradient extending across forms and substrates we are only beginning to map.

Crows neurally encoding their own perception. Bees playing. Octopuses remembering and avoiding pain. Sperm whales speaking in symbolic dialects. Slime molds solving traffic problems without a single nerve cell.

The question "Who is conscious?" will be the defining scientific and ethical question of the coming decades. The Cambridge Declaration raised it. The New York Declaration expanded it. The answers emerging from laboratories and oceans will radicalize it further.

## FAQ

**What does the Cambridge Declaration on Consciousness state?**
Signed in 2012, it states that humans are not the only beings with neurological substrates for consciousness. All mammals, all birds, and many other organisms including octopuses possess the basis for conscious states.

**What is the New York Declaration (2024)?**
An expansion confirming strong scientific evidence for consciousness in mammals and birds, and a realistic possibility for all vertebrates and many invertebrates. Signed by 40 leading experts including Koch, Seth, and Chalmers.

**Can bees really be conscious?**
Lars Chittka, a leading insect cognition researcher, estimates the probability at 80%. Bees demonstrate metacognition, play behavior, emotion-like states, and individual personality differences — all empirically documented.

**What is Integrated Information Theory?**
A mathematical theory of consciousness (Tononi et al., IIT 4.0, 2023) that quantifies consciousness as integrated information (Phi). It is substrate-independent and can in principle be applied to any system — not only human brains.

**Do plants have consciousness?**
Plants demonstrably exhibit habituation (Gagliano, 2014), associative learning (Gagliano, 2016), and acoustic signaling. Whether this constitutes consciousness is contested: Taiz et al. (2019) argued plant signaling doesn't require cognition. The debate remains open.
